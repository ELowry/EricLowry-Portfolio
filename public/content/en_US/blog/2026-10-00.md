A few months ago I started building this site in my spare time to replace my defunct WordPress portfolio/personal website. However, rather than just using some other, better CMS like [Statamic](https://statamic.com), I wanted this site to actually reflect what I can do, down to how it is coded.

So I started tinkering with the [lightweight static website framework](content/en_US/websites/lightweight-static/lightweight-static.md) I had been slowly building up over the years and jotted down ideas of what I could do to make it stand out.

I've done nearly as much game dev and design as web stuff over the past decade, so why not combine the two? Could I take inspiration from some of my favorite games and make something kind of unique?  
Though it's not nearly finished, [the site is mostly functional now](https://eric-lowry.com/), so I'll let you be the judge of that.

### Two Modes

Before I [get to the nitty-gritty↓](#_game-design), please indulge a brief tangent on something important that is too often overlooked: web accessibility.

Over 10 years ago, I met a team who were working on [Dragonium](https://www.dragonium.net/), a browser-based game that was designed to be fully functional for people with visual impairments. Though I found it cool at the time, I didn't really "get" what it represented from a technical perspective. Today, i am thoroughly impressed at what they achieved.

Today, I would consider myself somewhere towards the end of phase 3 of [Craig Abbot's "web accessibility Dunning-Kruger scale"](https://www.craigabbott.co.uk/blog/2026/the-dunning-kruger-effect-in-accessibility/) (great article), meaning I generally understand web accessibility best practices, but I usually have to go down a rabbit hole to actually make sure my code follows sensible "good practices".  
This means that I now know enough to confidently state that **I probably would not be able to make a web-based side-scorller fully viable for the visually impaired** or other browser accessibility feature users. I could make it "sort of" work, but it would likely be a slog to use.

So instead, I built the site around a shared content structure (a bunch of Markdown files and some code to structure them into a full website) that then gets displayed in two different "modes": the game mode, which I called "Exploration Mode" to avoid confusing any non-gamers, and a "Text Mode" that functions as a traditional website.

The thing is, websites have evolved to function the way they do for a reason. Rapid delivery of text-based and visual information on a 2D screen works best when sticking to standard web design logic. So what started as an accessibility feature is really a sensible "fallback" for anyone looking to read through my site without having to bother walking around 2D environments.  
About those…

### Game Design?

Once I got the technical structure behind all this in place, I started having to plan how I was going to "design" this "game":

- How do I "place" the website's content into 2D environments in a way that doesn't make things unwieldly and overly complicated to navigate?
- What gameplay mechanics do I want to build? Can I get away with simple horizontal movement and still make something that feels somewhat immersive and exciting enough to make people want to explore?
- Do I want to include sound, or is that bad for people simply expecting to browse a website?
- Are there games I can look at for inspiration? Are there counter-examples of games doing exactly this, and making it boring, frustrating, or simply bland?

So yeah, I guess I was working on game design… for a portfolio.  
But I don't want to spend a year building this site, so I had to focus on the most basic components of exploration: moving around and finding cool things.

If you've taken a look at the site at the time I'm writing this, I have simple movement taken care of in a way that works on any device/input method. On the other hand, I still need to do a lot before there's actually anything **worth exploring**.  
Yeah… for some reason, I decided I wanted to give pixel art a try for the first time and give each "page" and each "section" of my website custom art assets as a means of making exploration worthwhile.  
Is it a bad game design decision? Probably. But I don't care!

### Ceci n'est pas un pixel

I did _sort of_ learn how to draw when I studied architecture, but I wouldn't call myself an artist. But hey, there's only one way to learn.

I've tried drawing a few pixel art sprites in the past, and I have a decent enough understanding of how it all works to not make a total fool of myself, I guess, but I wanted this to make **some** visual sense. I mentioned in a previous post how I started by creating basic character animations, but the next step was to start creating an environment. For this, I had 3 requirements:

1. I needed to **define a limited color palette**; otherwise, I knew I would not be able to create a cohesive art style, especially as I only get to work on this site in my spare time and will likely change methods 3 times before anything is finished.
2. I had to ensure that **the character would remain clearly visible against any background** I drew. Meaning none of the colors could be too similar, but they still had to feel cohesive together.
3. I needed to **be able to draw important assets in isolation** and have it still make visual sense (again, because this would be a very progressive process).

So, step by step, I started building the visual identity that is now sarting to emerge on the site's home page.

#### Color Palette

I already had a color palette for the website defined, so I could extrapolate from that to easily create something somewhat limited, but that would still give me enough freedom to draw cool things:

- I took the background color and built a base gradient from that.
- I removed all colors on which the character would become confusing or difficult to see.
- I created slight variants that I could use to distinguish between "materials", including a slightly green-tinted version for plants and a slightly brown-tinted version for wood.
- I added the site's main accent color and a darker variant for shading.
- I then added 4 "splash" colors that I could use to make things stand out, or to create visual accents.

#### Test Assets

To test this out and make sure it would work well, I drew a few placeholder and "work in progress" assets, including basic doors and "construction" stuff (boxes, warning cones, etc.).

Surprisingly enough, drawing these came pretty easily. I wouldn't call them outstanding, but they do the job, fit in the site's aesthetic, and mostly communicate what they must.

Getting the doors to animate correctly took a few tries, and I ended up adding "light bleed" as they open/close to give things a bit more depth and physicality.
